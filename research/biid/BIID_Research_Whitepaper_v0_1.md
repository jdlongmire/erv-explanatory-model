# Biological Information Infrastructure Degradation (BIID)
## A Functional-Origin Hypothesis for Viral Delivery Systems and Endogenous Retroviral Remnants

**Research working paper v0.1 | 16 November 2026**  
**Status:** Hypothesis development; not peer reviewed.  
**Authorial framing:** A proposed originally beneficial, cross-organism genetic delivery infrastructure, followed by regulatory degradation.  

## Abstract
The Biological Information Infrastructure Degradation (BIID) hypothesis proposes that some present-day viral delivery mechanisms and endogenous retroviral (ERV) sequences may derive from an originally regulated, beneficial biological information-transfer infrastructure operating between organisms, potentially across species. The hypothesis separates (A) the proposed beneficial functional origin of delivery machinery from (B) the stronger claim that original cross-species delivery explains specific shared ERV genomic patterns. Demonstrated precedents include regulated bacteriophage-like gene-transfer agents, virus-derived placental functions, and horizontal retroviral transmission. None establishes BIID's proposed historical direction. Orthologous ERV insertion sites and nested mutation patterns remain a major evidentiary challenge. This paper sets out mechanistic requirements, rival explanations, discriminating predictions, and explicit rejection criteria.

## 1. Definitions and scope
**Delivery infrastructure:** A hypothetical coordinated system of genetic payload packaging, transport, recipient targeting, integration, and regulatory control.

**Degradation:** Loss or alteration of control, targeting, payload function, host compatibility, or replication constraints. Degradation may occur without simple loss of sequence complexity.

**ERV:** A genomic sequence of retroviral ancestry, generally resulting from germline integration and vertical inheritance. ERV classification is based on molecular signatures, not on whether a sequence is presently beneficial.

**BIID-A:** Some viral delivery machinery descends from previously beneficial, regulated information-transfer systems.

**BIID-B:** An ancestral cross-species distribution infrastructure plus inheritance and degradation explains a measurable subset of shared ERV loci better than conventional explanations.

**Scope limitation:** BIID is an unverified historical model. It does not establish that viruses were universally beneficial, that all ERVs had original host-beneficial functions, or that shared ERVs cease to be evidence of common ancestry.

## 2. Proposed architecture
The hypothetical original system has six functions: (1) payload specification; (2) packaging and protection; (3) host/recipient recognition; (4) delivery; (5) integration or regulated expression; and (6) validation and lifecycle control. In an initially good but normally aging ecosystem, the proposed purpose is maintenance and inter-organism genetic exchange. The model does not require indefinite organismal survival.

Possible degradation modes include loss of target specificity, excessive replication, incompatible recipient targeting, insertional disruption, regulatory escape, and host exploitation. These are plausible failure categories; their claimed shared historical origin remains unproven.

## 3. Primary experimental anchors
### 3.1 Regulated gene transfer
Lang and Beatty (2000) experimentally characterized the *Rhodobacter capsulatus* gene-transfer agent, which packages approximately 4.5-kb segments of host DNA and transfers them to recipient cells. CckA/CtrA signaling regulates its structural gene expression. The authors interpreted the system as a defective prophage adapted for bacterial genetic exchange. This supports regulated virus-like delivery and simultaneously supplies evidence for a virus-to-benefit historical direction, which BIID must address.

### 3.2 Functional retroviral-derived gene
Dupressoir et al. (2009) knocked out the mouse *syncytin-A* gene and found placental cell-fusion defects and embryonic lethality. This strongly demonstrates a necessary present-day function for a retrovirus-derived gene. It does not determine whether the ancestral viral machinery was initially beneficial.

### 3.3 Horizontal retroviral transmission
Yohn et al. (2005) mapped 287 PTERV1 integration sites across African great ape and Old World monkey genomes, finding approximately 95.8% at nonorthologous positions in their comparisons. This supports independently acquired integrations and shows that horizontal viral spread can be important. Later improved assemblies refine the insertion picture; PTERV1 cannot simply be used as evidence against vertical inheritance at other ERV loci.

### 3.4 Orthologous ERV evidence
Johnson and Coffin (1999) used shared integration-site polymorphisms and orthologous proviral sequences to reconstruct primate relationships. Exact locus correspondence, flanking sequence, target-site duplications, and shared derived mutations are jointly relevant. A distribution hypothesis must account for the whole pattern rather than sequence similarity alone.

## 4. Rival models
**M1: Conventional model.** Exogenous retroviral infection, germline integration, vertical inheritance, mutation, selection, lineage sorting, loss, and occasional horizontal viral transmission.

**M2: BIID distribution model.** Originally beneficial cross-organism genetic delivery with regulated integration; later degradation produces viral pathogenesis and retained genomic remnants.

**M3: Hybrid model.** Early regulated genetic exchange, followed by viral diversification and substantial vertical inheritance. M3 risks being empirically indistinguishable from M1 unless it yields unique molecular predictions.

## 5. Comparative predictions
| Test | M1 prediction | BIID-B must predict independently |
|---|---|---|
| Orthologous ERV loci | Strong correspondence within inherited lineages | Targeting rule generating precise repeated insertions, or acknowledged shared inheritance |
| Shared disabling mutations | Correlated derived changes through ancestry | Mechanism for same changes, with likelihood quantified |
| Target-site duplications | Retroviral integration signature | Same molecular boundary mechanism in proposed delivery infrastructure |
| Nonorthologous PTERV1 | Independent infections/expansions | Distinct predicted distribution from regulated transfer |
| Functional ERV sequences | Co-option and selection | Evidence that specific beneficial roles preceded pathogenic forms |
| Delivery machinery phylogeny | Viral-to-host recruitment often possible | Independent evidence for host-regulated-to-virus direction |

A model that merely accommodates each observation after it is seen has no discriminating value. BIID requires advance predictions and quantitative model comparison.

## 6. Proposed empirical protocol
1. **Pre-register loci and comparison species.** Sample characterized HERV-W, HERV-K (HML-2), and PTERV1 insertions in human, chimpanzee, gorilla, orangutan, and macaque genomes as appropriate. Avoid conflating nonorthologous family members.
2. **Acquire assembly-specific genomic coordinates.** Use current reference assemblies and orthology maps; record source accession and version. No genomic coordinate dataset has yet been assembled for this paper.
3. **Validate boundaries.** Annotate LTRs, gag/pol/env remnants, target-site duplications, flanking sequence, deletions, and possible assembly ambiguities.
4. **Quantify targeting.** Estimate integration-site preferences using appropriate sequence and chromatin controls; distinguish broad integration bias from repeated exact-base targeting.
5. **Compare genealogical predictions.** Reconstruct insertion presence/absence and shared derived substitutions; assess likelihood under inheritance versus independent targeting plus horizontal transfer.
6. **Reconstruct function.** Map demonstrated gene expression, knockout phenotypes, regulatory activity, and molecular ancestry. Distinguish current function from ancestral function.
7. **Assess complexity penalties.** Compare explanatory fit against number of additional unobserved mechanisms and free parameters.

## 7. Rejection and revision criteria
BIID-B should be rejected for any specified locus set if independent targeting cannot reproduce observed orthology and shared-mutation patterns within defensible parameter ranges, or if the model makes no prediction distinguishable from M1. BIID-A should be downgraded if proposed ancestral regulated machinery lacks any independent molecular trace or phylogenetic support. Failure of BIID-B does not logically refute every possible beneficial origin of delivery machinery; failure of BIID-A removes BIID's principal historical motivation.

## 8. Epistemic assessment
- **HIGH:** Regulated bacteriophage-like gene transfer exists (Lang and Beatty, 2000).
- **HIGH:** Some retrovirus-derived genes are necessary for host development (Dupressoir et al., 2009).
- **HIGH:** Orthologous ERVs provide evidence of inherited genomic history (Johnson and Coffin, 1999).
- **HIGH:** Some ERV distributions reflect distinct integration histories (Yohn et al., 2005).
- **UNCERTAIN:** The first delivery systems were generally beneficial.
- **UNCERTAIN:** BIID explains shared ERVs better than established ancestry models.

## Research integrity note
This is a structured hypothesis paper, not a completed systematic review or quantitative genomic test. The listed studies were checked through accessible primary article records; no locus-level data analysis or independent replication was performed. All historical claims specific to BIID remain conjectural pending tests.
